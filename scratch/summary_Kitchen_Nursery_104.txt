The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) monitors human exposure to bisphenols, most notably **bisphenol A (BPA)**, through the **National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES)**[1][2]. This data is published in the CDC’s *National Report on Human Exposure to Environmental Chemicals*[3][4].

### CDC Biomonitoring of Bisphenols

*   **Methodology:** The CDC assesses exposure by measuring the concentrations of these chemicals and their metabolites in urine samples collected from a nationally representative sample of the U.S. population[1][2][5]. Because bisphenols have short half-lives in the body, these measurements reflect recent, ongoing exposure[1][5].
*   **Widespread Exposure:** For years, the CDC has reported finding measurable levels of BPA in the urine of nearly all individuals tested[2], confirming that exposure to BPA among the U.S. population is widespread[6][2][5].
*   **Trends:** Recent data and studies have indicated that as the use of BPA has faced regulatory action and public concern, there have been signs of declining BPA levels[5][7][8].
*   **Bisphenol Alternatives:** As manufacturers have moved to replace BPA with alternatives, the CDC has expanded its research to monitor these substitutes, such as **bisphenol S (BPS)**, **bisphenol F (BPF)**, and **bisphenol AF (BPAF)**[7][8]. Studies have indicated that while human exposure to BPA may be declining, exposure to some alternatives like BPS has shown signs of increasing[7][8].

### Understanding the Data
*   **Exposure vs. Health Effects:** The CDC emphasizes that finding a measurable amount of a chemical in urine indicates exposure, but it does not, by itself, imply that the levels cause adverse health effects[2][7].
